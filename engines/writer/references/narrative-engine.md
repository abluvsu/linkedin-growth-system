# Narrative Engine

Every post must select one archetype before drafting. The archetype determines the story arc, pacing, and emotional trajectory. Mixing archetypes in a single post creates confusion — commit to one.

---

## Archetype 1: Transformation

**Story Arc:** Old belief → Discovery → New belief

### Structure (3 beats)

1. **Old belief**: State what you (or the audience) used to believe. Make it specific and relatable.
2. **Discovery**: Describe the event, conversation, or data point that broke the old belief. This is the hinge of the post.
3. **New belief**: Articulate the replacement belief clearly. The reader should be able to repeat it in one sentence.

### When to Use

- Career posts where you changed your mind about a professional practice.
- Leadership posts where a management philosophy shifted after real-world feedback.
- Personal brand posts where vulnerability and growth are the value.

### Example

> For years I believed the best way to grow on LinkedIn was to post every day. Quantity equals visibility, right? Then I looked at my analytics from Q1: my 3 most-shared posts were all written on days I almost didn't post because I had something specific to say. The 47 filler posts generated almost nothing. New rule: I only publish when I have an angle I'd defend in a room full of smart people. Frequency without substance is just noise with a schedule.

### Anti-Patterns

- **Vague "old me"**: Saying "I used to think differently" without specifying the old belief. The contrast needs to be concrete.
- **Missing discovery**: Jumping from old belief to new belief without showing what changed your mind. The discovery is the proof — without it, the post is just two opinions.
- **Humble-brag disguised as transformation**: "I used to be bad at sales, now I close £500k deals." The audience detects self-promotion wearing a growth costume.

## Archetype 2: Observation

**Story Arc:** Saw something → Pattern emerged → Lesson

### Structure (3 beats)

1. **Observation**: Describe what you saw, heard, or noticed. Ground it in a specific moment or data point.
2. **Pattern**: Connect the observation to a broader pattern. This is where the insight lives.
3. **Lesson**: Distil the pattern into a principle the reader can apply.

### When to Use

- Operations and process posts where you've spotted a recurring issue across teams or companies.
- AI / Tech posts where a product behaviour or adoption curve reveals something non-obvious.
- Any post where your professional vantage point gives you a cross-sectional view others lack.

### Example

> I reviewed 40 proposals from freelancers bidding on a client project last week. 36 of them opened with a description of their own experience. 4 opened with a diagnosis of the client's problem. Guess which 4 made the shortlist. The pattern is consistent across every hiring round I've run: the people who get hired are the ones who prove they understand the problem before they pitch the solution. Your credentials are the footnote, not the headline.

### Anti-Patterns

- **Observation without pattern**: Describing something interesting but failing to connect it to a broader truth. An anecdote alone is a diary entry, not a post.
- **Pattern without observation**: Stating a general principle without grounding it in something you actually saw. This becomes advice without evidence.
- **Over-generalising from one data point**: "I saw one thing, therefore the whole industry is changing." The pattern needs at least a few supporting signals to be credible.

## Archetype 3: Contrarian

**Story Arc:** Popular belief → Challenge → Evidence → New framing

### Structure (4 beats)

1. **Popular belief**: State the mainstream position clearly and fairly. Do not strawman it.
2. **Challenge**: Introduce the tension — why this belief might be incomplete or wrong.
3. **Evidence**: Support the challenge with a story, number, or pattern. This beat separates a contrarian post from a hot take.
4. **New framing**: Offer a replacement perspective the reader can adopt.

### When to Use

- Startup posts where conventional advice (raise fast, hire fast, scale fast) deserves pushback.
- Career posts where widely shared tips produce poor outcomes in practice.
- Any post where you have evidence that the popular position has a blind spot.

### Example

> Everyone says you should "build in public." Share your revenue, your failures, your roadmap. I did this for 18 months. Here's what actually happened: competitors copied two features I announced before launch, a potential acquirer used my public revenue numbers to lowball me, and my team felt exposed when I shared a hard quarter. Building in public is powerful — but the advice never mentions the cost. A better frame: share the lessons, not the ledger. Teach from your experience without handing over your strategy.

### Anti-Patterns

- **Contrarian for shock value**: Disagreeing without evidence just to stand out. "Unpopular opinion: meetings are great" is not a post, it's a provocation with no substance.
- **Strawmanning the popular belief**: Misrepresenting the mainstream position to make it easier to knock down. Sophisticated readers will notice and disengage.
- **No replacement framing**: Tearing down a belief without offering something better leaves the reader empty-handed. Always close with a constructive alternative.

## Archetype 4: Failure

**Story Arc:** Mistake → Consequence → Learning

### Structure (3 beats)

1. **Mistake**: Describe what you did wrong. Be specific about the decision, not just the outcome.
2. **Consequence**: Show what happened as a result. The stakes make the story worth reading.
3. **Learning**: Extract the transferable lesson. The reader should be able to apply it to their own context.

### When to Use

- Leadership posts where a management decision backfired and the reflection helps others.
- Startup posts where a strategic error (pricing, hiring, product scope) produced a clear consequence.
- Career posts where a professional misstep taught something the reader hasn't learned yet.

### Example

> I hired a senior engineer based entirely on a technical assessment. Perfect score. In the interview, I noticed they interrupted other panellists twice, but I dismissed it — the code was flawless. Within three months, two junior developers had asked to transfer teams. The senior engineer's technical output was exceptional but their communication style created a fear-based environment where juniors stopped asking questions. The consequence: our bug rate went up because the people closest to the code were afraid to flag problems. The lesson isn't "culture fit matters" — that's too vague. The lesson is: interview for how a person handles being wrong, not just how they perform when they're right.

### Anti-Patterns

- **Failure that's actually a win**: "I failed to take a vacation for 3 years and accidentally built a £2M business." The audience can tell when the failure is a trophy in disguise.
- **No real consequence**: If the mistake didn't cost anything, the story has no weight. "I sent an email with a typo" is not a failure post.
- **Over-dramatising**: Making a minor setback sound catastrophic undermines trust. Match the tone to the actual stakes.

## Archetype 5: Prediction

**Story Arc:** Trend → Implication → Action

### Structure (3 beats)

1. **Trend**: Identify a real, observable movement with evidence. Not a wish or a guess.
2. **Implication**: Explain what this trend means for the reader's work, career, or industry. This is the insight layer.
3. **Action**: Give the reader something concrete to do in response. Predictions without action are commentary.

### When to Use

- AI / Tech posts where a technology shift has downstream consequences for jobs, skills, or business models.
- Startup posts where market dynamics are changing and founders need to adjust strategy.
- Career posts where workforce trends demand proactive moves from professionals.

### Example

> Three of the largest consulting firms I work with have quietly started requiring AI proficiency in their hiring criteria for non-technical roles. Not "AI awareness" — actual prompt engineering and workflow automation skills. The implication: within 24 months, "I'm not technical" will stop being a neutral statement and start being a liability, the same way "I don't use email" became a liability in 2005. The action is simple but uncomfortable — pick one AI tool relevant to your function, spend 30 minutes a day using it for real work (not tutorials), and document what you learn. The professionals who do this now will be the ones training their teams in two years. The ones who wait will be the ones being retrained.

### Anti-Patterns

- **Prediction without evidence**: "I think X will happen" is an opinion, not a prediction. Ground it in signals you can point to.
- **Too far out**: Predictions for 2035 are unfalsifiable and feel like science fiction. Keep the horizon close enough that the reader can act now (6-24 months).
- **No actionable step**: A prediction that ends with "we'll see what happens" wastes the reader's attention. Always close with something the reader can do this week.
