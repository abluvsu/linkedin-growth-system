# Positioning Engine

Every post must pass through at least one lens before writing begins. The lens transforms a generic topic into an angle worth reading.

---

## Lens 1: Contrarian

**Core Question:** What does everyone believe? Why is it incomplete?

### Escalation

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Weak** | "Networking is important for your career." |
| **Good** | "Most networking advice is backwards — you shouldn't meet more people, you should go deeper with fewer." |
| **Exceptional** | "The best networkers I know never 'network.' They solve problems for 5 people so well that those 5 become their entire pipeline. The advice to 'expand your network' is a trap that optimises for quantity of shallow connections over density of trust." |

### When This Lens Works Best

- The mainstream take is so widely accepted that nobody questions it anymore.
- You have lived experience or data that contradicts the consensus.
- The topic has a vocal majority pushing one direction (e.g., "always say yes," "hustle harder," "AI will replace everyone").
- You can articulate *why* the popular belief persists despite being incomplete.

## Lens 2: Pattern Break

**Core Question:** What trend am I seeing repeatedly that nobody is talking about?

### Escalation

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Weak** | "I've noticed a lot of layoffs recently." |
| **Good** | "I've talked to 12 founders this quarter. Nine of them are hiring for the same role that didn't exist 18 months ago." |
| **Exceptional** | "Across 12 founder conversations this quarter, nine are hiring 'AI Integration Managers' — people who sit between engineering and operations. This role didn't exist 18 months ago. The pattern tells me something the job boards don't: companies aren't replacing people with AI, they're creating new roles to manage the seam between human judgement and automated output." |

### When This Lens Works Best

- You occupy a position where you see across multiple companies, industries, or teams.
- You have recent, specific observations (not hypotheticals).
- The pattern contradicts or complicates a dominant narrative.
- Readers would not have access to the same cross-sectional view.

## Lens 3: Founder Lens

**Core Question:** What would only someone in my position know?

### Escalation

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Weak** | "Running a business is hard." |
| **Good** | "The hardest part of running a solo consultancy isn't finding clients — it's saying no to the wrong ones when your bank account is thin." |
| **Exceptional** | "Last month I turned down a £15k project because the client wanted me to own a metric I couldn't control. My savings account did not appreciate the decision. But I've learned that the projects that destroy solo operators aren't the hard ones — they're the ones with misaligned accountability. You do great work, the metric doesn't move because of factors outside your scope, and now your reputation is collateral damage." |

### When This Lens Works Best

- The insight comes from a specific lived decision, not a general principle.
- Sharing the detail adds credibility that no amount of reasoning could.
- The audience is likely to face the same situation and would benefit from the honest version.
- The topic is one where public advice and private reality diverge sharply.

## Lens 4: Second-Order Effect

**Core Question:** What happens next that nobody is predicting?

### Escalation

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Weak** | "AI tools are getting better at writing code." |
| **Good** | "If AI writes most boilerplate code, junior developer roles will shrink — and that changes how we build engineering teams." |
| **Exceptional** | "Everyone is talking about AI replacing junior developers. Here's what nobody is asking: if juniors disappear, who becomes the next generation of senior engineers? The junior-to-senior pipeline is a 5-to-8-year apprenticeship. Cut the entry point and in a decade you don't have a junior developer problem — you have a senior developer crisis. Companies celebrating AI-driven headcount reduction today are unknowingly hollowing out their leadership bench for 2032." |

### When This Lens Works Best

- A major shift is underway and commentary is stuck on the first-order impact.
- You can trace the causal chain two or three steps further than the common take.
- The second-order effect is uncomfortable or counterintuitive.
- The timeline matters — the effect is delayed enough that few people are planning for it.

## Lens 5: Hidden Cost

**Core Question:** What is nobody measuring?

### Escalation

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Weak** | "Meetings waste a lot of time." |
| **Good** | "The cost of a one-hour meeting with 8 people isn't one hour — it's eight hours plus the context-switching cost for every attendee." |
| **Exceptional** | "I tracked every meeting at my last company for a quarter. The average 'quick sync' involved 6 people, lasted 38 minutes, and generated 2.4 hours of follow-up work per attendee. One weekly standup — supposedly 15 minutes — consumed 14.4 person-hours per week when you included prep, attendance, and downstream tasks. We never measured this. We measured project velocity, sprint points, and deployment frequency. The meeting tax was invisible because nobody owned it. The most expensive line item in your operating budget might be the one that never appears on a spreadsheet." |

### When This Lens Works Best

- There is a known pain point but the true magnitude is hidden or unmeasured.
- You can quantify or approximate the real cost with specifics.
- The audience is in a position to act on the information once they see it.
- The hidden cost compounds over time, making the reveal more urgent.

## Lens Selection Matrix

Use this matrix to pick your starting lens based on content type. You may combine lenses, but start with the preferred one.

| Content Type | Preferred Lens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Career | Founder Lens | Personal experience is the differentiator. Career advice is saturated; only specificity cuts through. |
| AI / Tech | Second-Order Effect | Everyone covers the news. Nobody covers what happens next. The audience craves foresight, not summary. |
| Leadership | Hidden Cost | Leaders care about what they can't see. Revealing invisible costs earns trust and signals strategic depth. |
| Startup | Contrarian | Founders challenge conventional wisdom. The audience self-selects for people willing to question defaults. |
| Operations | Pattern Break | Operators spot patterns others miss. Cross-company observations are rare and valuable. |

### How to Use the Matrix

1. Identify your content type.
2. Start with the preferred lens and draft the core angle.
3. If the angle feels flat, try a secondary lens from the table.
4. If no single lens produces a strong angle, combine two (e.g., Contrarian + Hidden Cost: "Everyone says X is good, but nobody is measuring the cost of X").
5. If the topic cannot pass any lens, the topic is not ready. Research more or choose a different topic.
